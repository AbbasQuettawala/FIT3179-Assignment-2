Online video reaches almost every Australian adult. Yet streaming’s growth has not produced one shared way of watching and listening. Age and place reveal different combinations of services, with television and radio still part of the mix.

If almost everyone watches online, what makes our media habits different?

The 95 filled tiles establish a shared starting point, but they leave most of the story untold. Someone who watched a single video and someone who streams every day both count. To understand the difference, follow the shift in weekly viewing, compare the habits of different ages, then consider listening, news and place. Widespread use does not mean everyone watches in the same way.

How viewing has changed

Streaming grew
Free-to-air TV stayed

In 2017, weekly free-to-air TV use was far more common than paid streaming. By 2025, that order had reversed. But more than half of adults still watched free-to-air TV each week. Begin with the scale of the change, then look at the exception that makes this more than a story of replacement.

Streaming grew, and television moved online too

+38.4percentage points in weekly paid streaming use, 2017–2025

Each row shows change from its first year. Every darker band adds 10 percentage points; + means growth and − means a fall.

Paid streaming made the biggest gain, but catch-up TV grew too. Broadcasters were also reaching people online, even as weekly free-to-air viewing fell overall. This matters because the move online includes familiar television programmes as well as newer services. The shorter video trend begins in 2022, so its smaller change covers fewer years. The next chart shows why even the long-term shift was not a one-way journey.

Source: ACMA QF4, weekly use, 2017–2025. Free-to-air TV included self-recorded shows until 2021. Short videos start in 2022. Change is measured in percentage points, not percentage growth. [1]

Free-to-air TV still reaches half of adults

Follow the years along the path. Right means more paid streaming use; up means more free-to-air TV use.

The final turn in the path is the important exception. Free-to-air TV use rose from about 47% in 2024 to 52% in 2025, while paid streaming remained close to 68%. Streaming now leads, but broadcast television still has a substantial audience. These overlapping audiences show why the change is better understood as a shifting mix of formats than as everyone abandoning one for the other.

Source: ACMA QF4, 2017–2025. Use in the past seven days. [1]

Streaming and television now share the national audience. The next question is how that mix changes from one age group to another.

Seven age groups, different habits

The national average hides
different media habits

The national shift tells us which formats grew. It does not tell us who uses them. Compare services across ages, then look at weekly habits and viewing time. Together, these views show why two people who both watch online can have very different media routines.

YouTube spans generations; TikTok skews younger

Compare age groups across each service’s row. Darker cells mean more use in the past six months; labels show rounded percentages.

The age gap depends on the service. About 74% of 18–24-year-olds used YouTube, but so did 52% of adults aged 75 and above. For TikTok, the same comparison is 50% against just 1%. Calling both groups “online video users” hides that difference. Some services reach across generations, while others draw much more heavily on younger audiences. Streaming is moving onto the television screen

Compare each device’s 2021 and 2025 endpoints. An upward line means more use; hover or pin a device to follow it.

Going online does not necessarily mean watching on a phone. Smart-TV use for online video at home rose from about 47% to 62%, moving ahead of mobile phones. Computer use fell while tablet use barely changed. The television screen remains central even as the way content reaches it changes. People can use several devices, so this shows a changing mix of screens rather than individual viewers replacing one device with another.

Source: ACMA QF1, devices used to watch online video at home, 2021 and 2025. Multiple devices could be selected. [1]

Listening and keeping informed

Different formats fit
different parts of the day

The same variety extends beyond video. Listening differs by age and setting, with streaming and broadcast radio both finding an audience. News adds a different distinction: using information online tells us how widely it reaches people, but not whether they trust it.

Music streaming and radio reach different age mixes

Read across each audio format to compare ages. Darker cells mean more weekly use; labels show rounded percentages.

The contrast seen in video appears in listening too. Music streaming reaches about 54% of 18–24-year-olds but 11% of adults aged 75 and above. AM radio shows the reverse pattern, at about 8% and 44%. FM radio crosses that divide, with listeners in every age group. Treating audio as one audience would miss these differences, and age is only part of the picture: listening also varies by setting.

Source: ACMA QH3, 2025; listening in the past seven days. The video-service heatmap uses six months, so the two grids do not measure the same frequency. DAB+ is broadcast digital radio. [1]

FM radio travels with its listeners

Follow a format to its listening locations. Thicker links mean a larger share of that format’s listeners; hover or pin a format.

About 90% of FM listeners listen while travelling, compared with 30% at home. Music streaming is common in both settings among its own listeners. This gives the age patterns another dimension: different formats fit into different parts of the day. It also helps explain why one overall listening total is insufficient. These links compare settings within each format, rather than showing which format has the largest audience.

Source: ACMA QH16, 2025. Percentages use listeners of each format as their base, not all adults. People can listen in several places, so links overlap and do not add to 100%. [1]

Listening shows how media fits into everyday settings. Turning to news adds another question: when information reaches people online, how much confidence do they place in it?

Online news use spans ages; trust is another question

94%used online news and information in the six months before the 2025 survey

Each dot shows an age group’s use of online news and information in the past six months. Further right means more use.

Unlike TikTok’s steep age divide, online news and information use remains high across the age groups in this survey. Even among respondents aged 75 and above, about 89% report using it. Finding information online is therefore a shared activity, but it tells us little about confidence in what people find. Taking part online

Place adds another layer
to the media mix

Age is not the only difference hidden by national figures. City and regional audiences also have different habits. Start with that comparison, then look at digital inclusion and broadcast sites. The maps describe the conditions around media use, rather than explaining what caused any individual’s choices.

Broadcast media has a stronger regional audience

Each pair compares weekly use in city and regional groups. A wider horizontal gap means a larger difference.

Regional respondents are about eight percentage points more likely to use free-to-air TV and FM radio than city respondents. Yet paid video is common in both groups, and podcast use is almost equal. Location changes the balance without creating a simple online/offline divide. To put those habits in context, the next maps look at digital inclusion and broadcast sites. They cannot tell us what caused the survey differences.

Source: ACMA QF4 and QH3, 2025; use in the past week. City (“Metro”) and regional groups are not adjusted for age or income and differ from ADII’s geographic groups. Small gaps may reflect who was surveyed. [1]

Digital inclusion varies across the country

Darker states have higher scores for internet access, affordability and digital skills. Scores are out of 100, not percentages.

Taking part online involves more than choosing a service. The ACT has the highest state or territory score here, at 79.5, while Tasmania has the lowest at 69.4. These averages describe different conditions for participation, not what residents watch. Both places are small on the map, making the contrast easy to miss. Digital inclusion describes the ability to take part online. Broadcast radio has a different physical network. Compare where DAB+ digital-radio sites and AM/FM sites are registered to see how their geographic footprints differ.

Digital-radio sites cluster around cities

The simplified map shows where sites sit across Australia. Each circle marks a licensed digital-radio site; larger areas mean more licence records at that site. Choose a city for a closer view.

Radio’s digital formats still need physical broadcast sites. The DAB+ register contains a small network of sites clustered around cities, a different pattern from the widespread online use that opened this story. Larger circles show several licence records sharing a site, not stronger signals. Comparing this pattern with AM and FM next makes the geography of the two broadcast networks easier to see.

Sources: ACMA transmitter register, DR sheet, 13 July 2026 [6]; ABS boundaries [5]. Issued licences only. Circle area represents records per site, not signal coverage or confirmed operation.

AM and FM sites extend beyond the city clusters

The simplified map shows where sites sit across Australia. One equal-sized dot represents one licensed site. Colour identifies AM, FM or both; choose a city to separate nearby sites.

AM and FM sites appear across a much wider range of locations than the DAB+ clusters. Broadcast media therefore has a physical presence well beyond the major cities, alongside its continuing role in the regional survey results. That is useful context, not evidence that a particular site determines someone’s habits. An empty-looking area is not automatically unserved: transmitters can reach beyond their dots, and this map does not measure reception.

The takeaway

One online majority
Many different media routines

The opening 95-in-100 figure was true, but incomplete. Streaming grew while free-to-air TV kept a substantial audience. YouTube reached across ages while TikTok skewed younger. Listening changed with age and setting, and finding news online did not establish trust.

Place added another layer: regional audiences used more broadcast media, digital inclusion varied, and radio networks had different footprints. The lesson is to look beyond whether people are online. Understanding an audience means asking which services they use, how much time they spend, and where those habits fit into their lives.
