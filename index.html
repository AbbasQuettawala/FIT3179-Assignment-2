01 / How viewing has changed

Streaming grew.
Free-to-air TV stayed.

In 2017, weekly free-to-air TV viewing was far more common than paid streaming. By 2025, the order had reversed. Streaming had grown, but free-to-air TV still reached about half of Australian adults each week.

Weekly viewing · horizon chart

Paid streaming grew the most

+38.4 percentage points from 2017 to 2025

TV viewing can move online rather than disappear. Paid streaming grew while free-to-air viewing fell, and catch-up TV also grew. Each darker band adds 10 percentage points of change. In the legend, + means growth and − means a fall. Short videos are measured from 2022, not 2017, so their change covers a shorter time.

Source: ACMA QF4, 2017–2025. Use in the past seven days. Until 2021, the free-to-air TV figure also included shows people recorded themselves; the later figures do not. A rise from 20% to 30%, for example, is a rise of 10 percentage points. [1]

Streaming overtakes free-to-air TV

A national trend is not the same as one person switching. Following the line right and down shows more adults using paid streaming and fewer watching free-to-air TV. People can use both; the chart does not track viewers moving between services.

Source: ACMA QF4, 2017–2025. Use in the past seven days. [1]

02 / Seven age groups, different habits

Different ages,
different media mixes.

Younger adults often use short online videos, paid streaming and streamed music. Older adults spend more time watching free-to-air TV. Explore the charts to see how these habits differ by age.

Age groups mix old and new media. Younger adults use more streamed music and short videos, while older adults use more TV and radio. Select an age group to compare its weekly habits. This is a snapshot of different people, not a prediction of how their habits will change as they get older.

03 / Listening and keeping informed

What we listen to.
Which news we trust.

Younger adults are more likely to stream music, while radio remains popular among older adults and while travelling. People of all ages find news online. Using it does not necessarily mean trusting it.

Listening by age · heatmap

Different ages tune in differently

There is no single listening habit shared by everyone. Younger adults are more likely to stream music, while AM radio is more common among older adults. Grouping all listening together would hide these differences. The survey shows what people use, not why they choose it.

Source: ACMA QH3, 2025. Listening in the past seven days. DAB+ is digital radio broadcast over the air, not internet radio. Hover or tap to see how many people answered each question. [1]

Listening locations · network

FM radio is often heard on the move

Where people listen matters too. FM radio is closely linked with travelling, while streamed audio is used in a wider mix of places. Choose a format to highlight its three lines. A thicker line means more of that format’s listeners use it there, not that more people listen to it overall.

Source: ACMA QH16, 2025. Each percentage is based on listeners of that format. Someone can listen in several places, so the lines do not represent separate groups and must not be added together. [1]

04 / Taking part online

Access means more
than a connection.

People also need affordable internet and the skills to use it. Australia’s digital inclusion score is 73.6 out of 100, but the barriers people face differ from place to place.

5.9

points separate the digital inclusion scores for capital cities and regional areas.

The Australian Digital Inclusion Index (ADII) scores internet access, affordability and digital skills from 0 to 100. A higher score means fewer barriers to taking part online. A state’s average score does not tell us what people watch or listen to.

Map 1 · Shaded states

Where is it easier to take part online?

A bigger state does not always mean more people. Large areas draw the eye, while Tasmania’s lower score and the ACT’s higher score can be harder to spot. The next map uses population to set the size of each state’s circle, making smaller places easier to compare.

Sources: ADII 2025, using data collected in 2024 [3]; ABS map boundaries [5]. Scores are out of 100, not percentages. Both inclusion maps use the same colour scale, from 68 to 80. This type of shaded map is called a choropleth.

Map 2 · Population circles

Size shows people, not land

Population gives a different picture from land area. NSW and Victoria stand out because they have more residents than the much larger areas of WA and the NT. Colour still shows how easy it is to take part online, on average. It does not count how many people are left out.

Sources: ADII 2025, using 2024 data [3]; ABS March 2026 population [7]. In this circle map (a Dorling cartogram), circle area grows in proportion to population. The outlined circle shows one million people at the same scale. Circles are moved slightly to avoid overlap, so positions are not exact. Size and colour describe different years. Population includes all ages, not just adult media users.

City and regional habits · dumbbell chart

Regional audiences use more free-to-air TV and FM

Regional does not mean offline. Regional participants report more free-to-air TV and FM listening, yet podcast use is similar in both groups. The chart does not adjust for differences in age, income or other factors, so these gaps do not tell us why habits differ.

Source: ACMA QF4 and QH3, 2025. Use in the past week, using ACMA’s city (“Metro”) and “Regional” groups [1]. Hover or tap for participant counts and the regional percentage minus the city percentage. Small gaps may reflect who was surveyed. These groups differ from the areas used in the digital inclusion study.

Radio needs broadcast sites too. The next two maps show licensed transmitter sites. They do not show who is listening, where a signal can be received or whether a transmitter is operating. DAB+ is digital radio broadcast over the air, rather than through an internet connection.

Map 3 · Sized circles

Digital-radio sites cluster around cities

Digital radio still needs transmitters. Registered DAB+ sites cluster around a small number of cities. A bigger circle means more licence records at that site, not more listeners or better reception. Choose a city for a closer view; these records cannot tell us whether a particular home receives a signal.

Sources: ACMA Broadcast Transmitter Excel, DR sheet, 13 July 2026 [6]; ABS boundaries [5]. Only issued licences are included, grouped by site ID. Circle area grows in proportion to the number of records: two records use twice the area of one. This is a proportional-symbol map.

Map 4 · Dot map

AM and FM sites are spread more widely

AM and FM sites extend well beyond the city clusters above. Their wider spread adds background to the story of radio use, but cannot explain the city–regional listening gap on its own. Each dot is a registered site, not a listener. A blank area does not necessarily mean there is no reception.

Sources: ACMA Broadcast Transmitter Excel, AM and FM sheets, 13 July 2026 [6]; ABS boundaries [5]. Issued licences only. Records with the same site ID share one dot. Different IDs stay separate, even at the same location. Choose a city to see nearby sites more clearly.

The takeaway

Australians do not all follow the same feed.

Streaming is common, but TV, radio and online services still play different roles. Age, location and the ability to get online are all part of the story.

Where the data comes from

ACMA is the Australian Communications and Media Authority. Its survey results are adjusted, or “weighted”, to better represent Australian adults. A person can use several services, so the percentages do not form a single total. ADII is the Australian Digital Inclusion Index: a higher score means better internet access, affordability and digital skills.

  1. ACMA: How we watch and listen to content (Excel). Survey year: 2025. Report released 5 March 2026; the file’s web address is dated February. Earlier surveys provide the 2017–2025 trends. The questions cover weekly viewing (QF4), average hours for each format (QF5), video services used in six months (QF7), devices used at home (QF1), weekly listening (QH3) and listening locations (QH16). Workbook proportions are multiplied by 100 to show percentages. Participant counts come from “Column n”, not the number of people who answered yes. Age and city/regional groups come from the PBI BANNER sheets. Rankings use the same eight services each year. The horizon chart compares each activity with its first measured year. Free-to-air TV included self-recorded shows until 2021. People could choose several answers; small differences may reflect who was surveyed. See ACMA’s copyright policy and any notices on the source files.
  2. ACMA: How we use the internet (Excel). Survey year: 2025; published February 2026. Question QD8 asks about online news and information in the past six months. The chart keeps the survey’s rules about who was asked and uses the participant count for each age group. Percentages are weighted, rather than calculated directly from participant counts. Using online information does not necessarily mean reading news daily or trusting it. See ACMA’s copyright policy.
  3. RMIT / Swinburne / Telstra: Australian Digital Inclusion Index 2025 (PDF). Data collected in 2024. State scores and the capital-city/regional comparison are on page 11; the method is on page 10. Scores run from 0 to 100. They are not percentages and do not measure media use. State scores are copied from the source, not averaged to make a new national score. The way affordability is measured changed in 2025, so this page does not compare scores with earlier editions. See the official dashboard and its CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence terms.
  4. University of Canberra: Digital News Report: Australia 2026. The three trust figures describe news people use, news on social media and news from AI chatbots. They come from this report’s survey of Australian news consumers, not the ACMA participants in the age chart. They are shown separately to avoid mixing the two surveys. See the report for permission to reuse its findings.
  5. Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS): ASGS 2026 state and territory boundaries. Released 22 July 2026. Maps include the eight states and territories, but not the ABS category “Other Territories”. The outlines are simplified at 0.05 degrees. Mapshaper 0.6.113 repairs the outlines around NSW and the ACT, removes small errors, keeps 30% of the outline points and saves them as TopoJSON map files. The geographic maps use Australian Albers equal-area projection, which keeps land areas in proportion. Labels are placed for readability; the ACT label has a line pointing to its location. Population circles are moved to avoid overlap. These simplified maps are for display, not precise measurements of individual sites. See ABS copyright and credit requirements.
  6. ACMA: licensed broadcast transmitter register (download the Excel files as a ZIP). Data dated 13 July 2026. Only records marked “Issued” in the AM, FM and DR sheets are used, within the eight states and territories. Temporary licences are excluded. Duplicate day/night entries are removed using the band, licence, technical specification and site ID; records are then grouped by site ID. Coordinates in degrees, minutes and seconds (GDA94) are converted to decimal degrees, including entries rounded to 60 seconds. Small differences between coordinate systems are not visible at this map scale. No signal coverage is calculated, and a licence does not prove a transmitter is operating. Population includes all ages in the eight states and territories, excluding “Other Territories”. Circle areas, not widths, grow in proportion to population; circles are moved apart so they do not overlap. How to read the numbers

Survey results are adjusted to better represent the population, then rounded for display. Tooltips show how many people answered the question. Missing years are left out, not shown as zero. People can choose more than one answer, so percentages must not be added. State scores describe internet access, affordability and skills, not the reasons for someone’s media choices.
