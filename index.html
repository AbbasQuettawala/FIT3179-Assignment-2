Streaming has become ordinary. What Australians watch and hear has not become uniform: the mix varies by age, habit and access.

01 / The great unbundling

The television did not disappear.
Its centre of gravity moved.

In 2017, free-to-air television reached seven in ten Australians each week while paid streaming reached fewer than three in ten. By 2025, those positions had crossed.

Horizon chart · 10-point bands

Paid streaming made the largest climb

+38.4 points from 2017 to 2025

The transition is uneven: paid streaming expands while broadcast viewing contracts, but catch-up services also grow. That combination suggests a shift in delivery rather than the disappearance of television content. Darker bands fold another 10 percentage points into a row; green means an increase and pink a decrease. Short-form video starts in 2022, so its shorter window is not directly comparable with the full-period changes.

Source: ACMA QF4, 2017–2025. Each measure covers the past seven days. The free-to-air definition included self-recorded content through 2021; interpret the longer trend with that change in mind. [1]

Connected scatterplot

The crossover, traced year by year

Opposite national trends do not prove individual switching. Moving right and down shows streaming becoming more widespread while free-to-air reach falls. People can use both, so this path is not a count of viewers moving from one service to another.

Source: ACMA, QF4, 2017–2025. Past-seven-day use. [1]

02 / Seven generations, seven mixes

Different ages,
different media mixes.

Younger Australians combine social video, subscriptions and streamed audio. Older adults spend more time watching free-to-air television. Change the format below to see how the age pattern shifts.

Age changes the mix, not simply how “digital” someone is. The younger profile favours streamed music and short-form video, while the older profile leans towards broadcast formats. 03 / Ears, eyes and trust

Audio travels with us.
Trust does not.

Music streaming is more common among younger adults; radio remains prominent later in life and in transit. Online news and information reach extends across ages, but access and trust are different questions.

Audio age heatmap

The dial and the playlist trade places

“Audio audience” hides distinct listening habits. Streamed music is stronger among younger adults, whereas AM radio has a much older audience. Treating all audio as one market would obscure the different groups reached by each delivery format; this survey does not identify the reasons behind their choices.

Source: ACMA QH3, listened in previous seven days, 2025. Base n available on hover. [1]

Bipartite network

Radio is closely tied to travel

Listening context helps distinguish otherwise similar formats. FM’s strong connection with travel contrasts with the broader locations used for streamed audio. This makes “where people listen” useful alongside “what they use”, but does not prove that commuting causes a format preference. Choose “Trace a format” to follow just its three links and reveal each location’s percentage. Width uses that format’s listeners as the base; links overlap and are not flows of people.

Source: ACMA QH16, 2025. Location percentages use each format’s listeners as the base; they are not parts of a whole. [1]

News use and trust

Online reach is high. Confidence is conditional.

94% accessed news and information online in 2025

Online news and information reaches beyond younger audiences. Even the oldest groups report widespread use, so the sharper age differences in entertainment should not be generalised to every online activity. This question measures access at least once in six months—not daily news use, preferred brands or trust.

04 / Access sets the ceiling

Before a feed can compete
a connection must work.

Content choice sits on top of affordability, connectivity and digital ability. Australia’s national inclusion score is 73.6, but place still changes the room people have to participate.

5.9

index points separate capital-city and regional digital inclusion scores.

Digital inclusion combines access, affordability and digital ability on a 0–100 scale. A higher score means fewer barriers. State averages describe access conditions; they do not measure media consumption or explain individual preferences.

Map 1 · Choropleth

Digital inclusion across physical Australia

Land area can dominate the impression of inequality. A large state occupies more ink without necessarily representing more people. Tasmania’s lower score and ACT’s higher score are easy to overlook geographically; the population cartogram changes the visual emphasis without changing the index values.

Sources: ADII 2025, 2024 fieldwork [3]; ABS boundaries [5]. Scores are on a 0–100 index, not percentages. Both inclusion maps use the same 68–80 colour domain.

Give people—not land—the space

Population changes which places dominate the picture. NSW and Victoria gain prominence because they contain many more residents than the geographically larger western and northern jurisdictions. Colour still describes inclusion conditions, not how many people are excluded. This view deliberately separates the scale of a population from its average index score.

Paired comparison · Metro and regional Australia

Broadcast habits remain stronger outside the metros

The geographic difference depends on the medium. Regional respondents report more free-to-air and FM use, while the metro–regional podcast estimates sit much closer together. That makes a blanket “regional audiences are offline” claim misleading. These descriptive gaps do not control for age, income or other population differences, and no significance claim is made.

Source: ACMA QF4 and QH3, 2025, past-week use; source-defined Metro/Regional groups [1]. Tooltips give both respondent bases and regional-minus-metro percentage-point gaps. This geography is not a state breakdown or an ADII remoteness classification.

Supporting context: broadcast radio also depends on physical infrastructure. The next two maps show licensed facilities—not listening figures, signal coverage, reception quality or proof that a transmitter is operating. DAB+ broadcast radio is distinct from internet radio and streaming.

Map 3 · Proportional symbols

Digital radio has a concentrated physical footprint

“Digital” does not always mean delivered over the internet. DAB+ relies on broadcast sites, which cluster around a relatively small set of urban areas in this register. Larger circles mean more licence records at a site, not a larger audience or stronger reception. Use the city views to separate nearby facilities; the register cannot tell us whether a particular household can receive them.

Map 4 · Dot map

AM/FM facilities extend beyond the capitals

AM/FM infrastructure is more geographically dispersed than DAB+ sites. The inland facilities are an important contrast with the urban concentrations in the preceding map. This gives context to the continued role of broadcast formats, but cannot explain the metro–regional listening gap by itself. One dot represents a real registered site, not a randomly placed listener; empty areas do not necessarily lack reception.

The takeaway

The future of Australian media is not one platform winning.

It is a common digital layer carrying very different habits—and an access gap deciding who can take part fully.

Method & ethics

Survey estimates are weighted and rounded for display. “Base n” is the respondent denominator. Survey waves in which a selected category was not measured are omitted. Multiple-response answers are separate percentages and must not be added. State inclusion scores are contextual evidence, not a causal explanation of media habits.
